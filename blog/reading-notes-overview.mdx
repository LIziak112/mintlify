I've always read a lot, but for most of my life I read passively — absorbing words, enjoying the ride, and then watching the ideas quietly dissolve over the following weeks. At some point I realized that reading without reflection is a little like cooking a meal and not eating it. The effort goes in, but you don't get the nourishment. So I started keeping reading notes. Not as a chore or a productivity ritual, but as a way to crystallize what I'm actually learning, to spot patterns and contradictions across very different books, and to build a personal library of ideas I can return to. This collection is that library — imperfect, evolving, and entirely my own.

## How I Take Notes

My process has changed a lot over the years and I expect it will keep changing. What I've landed on is simple enough to stick to but deliberate enough to produce something useful.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Read actively, pen in hand">
    I underline sentences that surprise me, write question marks next to things I don't buy, and scribble short reactions in the margins. The goal isn't to annotate everything — it's to stay in a conversation with the author rather than just receiving what they say.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Write a one-paragraph summary immediately after finishing">
    Within a day of finishing a book, I write a single paragraph that captures its core argument in my own words. No quotes, no notes to refer back to — just what stuck. This forces me to confront how much I actually understood.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Extract 3–5 key ideas">
    I go back through my annotations and pull out the ideas that feel most generative — the ones that seem to connect to other things I know, or that challenge something I believed. I write each one out as a short paragraph, not a bullet point, because sentences force more precision than fragments.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Revisit one month later and write a reflection">
    A month out, I re-read my notes without re-reading the book. Then I write a short reflection: what has changed in how I see the ideas now that I've had time to live with them? Which ones held up? Which ones felt thinner than I first thought? This is usually where the most honest thinking happens.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Books on My Mind

These are the books currently living in my head — the ones I keep referencing in conversation, returning to in my notes, or seeing echoed in everything else I read.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Thinking, Fast and Slow" icon="brain" href="/blog/book-notes">
    Daniel Kahneman — A deep, unsettling look at the two systems driving human thought and the cognitive biases that quietly steer our decisions.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The Art of Learning" icon="book-open" href="/blog/book-notes">
    Josh Waitzkin — A chess prodigy and martial arts champion unpacks the inner process of mastering any discipline, and what it really takes to perform under pressure.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Gödel, Escher, Bach" icon="atom" href="/blog/book-notes">
    Douglas Hofstadter — A mind-bending exploration of self-reference, consciousness, and formal systems, woven together through music, art, and mathematics.
  </Card>
  <Card title="The Design of Everyday Things" icon="lightbulb" href="/blog/book-notes">
    Don Norman — A classic argument that bad design blames the user when it should blame itself — and a practical lens for thinking about how humans interact with the world.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

<Note>
  Everything here is a personal interpretation. These notes reflect how I read these books, at the time I read them, through the particular lens of my own experiences and preoccupations. They are not comprehensive reviews or academic summaries — they are thinking-out-loud. If they're useful to you too, that's a bonus.
</Note>
