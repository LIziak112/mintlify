Every time I've genuinely learned something — not skimmed it, not nodded along to it, but actually absorbed it into the way I think — it has felt, at some point during the process, like failure. Like I was bad at it. Like maybe this particular thing wasn't for me. That sensation of friction, of the mind straining against something that won't quite fit, is deeply unpleasant. It's also, I've come to believe, the sensation of learning actually working. The discomfort isn't a sign that something is wrong. It's evidence that something is right.

## Desirable Difficulties

Cognitive psychologists Robert Bjork and Elizabeth Bjork coined the term "desirable difficulties" to describe a counterintuitive finding: learning conditions that make performance harder in the short term tend to produce stronger, more durable memory in the long term. Spacing practice out over time rather than massing it together. Testing yourself before you feel ready. Interleaving different topics rather than drilling one thing to exhaustion. All of these feel worse while you're doing them. All of them work dramatically better.

The mechanism makes a certain intuitive sense once you see it. When retrieval is easy — when you just read something again and it feels familiar — your brain doesn't need to do much work. The information glides by without leaving a deep trace. But when retrieval is hard, when you have to strain and search and sometimes fail before finding the answer, your brain encodes the memory more robustly. The struggle is the mechanism, not the obstacle to it.

This reframing changed how I approach studying. I stopped re-reading notes and started closing them and trying to recall what I'd read. I stopped reviewing things the same day and started scheduling reviews for when I'd already half-forgotten the material. It felt inefficient. My test performance on any given session looked worse. But over weeks, the retention was incomparably better.

## The Illusion of Understanding

There's a particular trap that catches almost everyone: the feeling of understanding that comes from reading something clearly written. A well-crafted explanation goes down smooth. The ideas feel obvious. You finish the chapter thinking: *I've got this.* Then someone asks you to explain it, and you reach for the words and find — almost nothing. A vague outline where a working model should be.

This is what psychologist Robert Cialdini calls the "illusion of knowing" and what learning researchers call fluency illusion: the ease of processing a well-presented idea gets mistaken for genuine comprehension. Recognition feels like knowledge. It isn't.

Active recall is the antidote. Not highlighting, not rereading, not making beautiful notes — actually closing the book and asking yourself: what did I just learn? What are the key claims? How do they connect? What would I need to explain this to someone who knows nothing about it? The answers reveal, with uncomfortable precision, exactly what you do and don't understand.

## My Learning Stack

Over the years I've settled on a set of methods that have consistently produced results, and a set of tools that support them.

<Tabs>
  <Tab title="Techniques">
    **Spaced repetition** is the practice of reviewing material at increasing intervals — a day later, then three days, then a week, then a month. It exploits the spacing effect to lock information into long-term memory with minimal review time. I use it for anything I want to retain: vocabulary, concepts, facts, code syntax.

    **Teaching out loud** is exactly what it sounds like — explaining something to an actual person, or to an imaginary one, in plain language without notes. The act of teaching is the most reliable test of understanding I know. Gaps and confusions that feel invisible when you're reading become glaringly obvious the moment you have to narrate.

    **Building small projects** is how I learn anything technical or creative. Reading about a concept gives me vocabulary; building something with it gives me intuition. I deliberately pick projects small enough to finish in a day or two — the goal is application, not completion. A half-built thing that taught me something real is worth more than a polished thing I built by following instructions I didn't understand.

    **The Feynman technique** involves writing an explanation of a concept as if you're teaching it to a curious twelve-year-old. Wherever the explanation breaks down or reaches for jargon, that's where real learning needs to happen. Then go back to source material, fill the gap, and revise the explanation until it's genuinely clear.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Tools">
    **Anki** is the gold standard for spaced repetition. It's not glamorous — it's a flashcard app with a scheduling algorithm underneath. But that algorithm is grounded in decades of memory science, and the discipline of making cards forces you to distill information into its testable core.

    **Obsidian** is where I keep my notes, but I use it less as a reference library and more as a thinking environment. I write notes in my own words, link related ideas manually, and review them periodically to find unexpected connections. The act of writing forces compression, and compression forces clarity.

    **A physical notebook** for first-pass notes during reading or listening. There's something about the slowness of handwriting that filters for what actually matters — you can't write fast enough to transcribe, so you have to decide what's worth capturing.

    **Voice memos** for rubber duck sessions. When I'm stuck on a concept, I'll record myself trying to explain it. Hearing my own confusion played back is brutally effective at identifying exactly where my understanding breaks down.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

## Struggle as Signal

I want to end with a reframe that has genuinely changed my relationship to learning. Struggle isn't a sign that you're failing — it's a sign that you're at the edge of your current understanding, which is precisely where growth happens. The frustration of not-yet-knowing is information: this is where to invest attention, this is where something interesting is waiting.

The students I've watched learn fastest aren't the ones who find things easy. They're the ones who've learned to interpret difficulty as direction rather than verdict. They lean into the friction instead of retreating from it. That orientation — treating struggle as signal — might be the single most leveraged learning habit of all.
